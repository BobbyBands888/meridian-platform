# The {region} Home Buyer Checklist

This is general information, not legal, financial, or pricing advice. {brand} connects buyers, sellers, and local pros; we are not a broker, we don't hold money, and we don't write contracts. Hire your own real estate attorney.

## Section 1 — Get ready

1. Set an all-in monthly budget. {#budget} Price is only part of it: add property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, and HOA dues, then decide the monthly number you're comfortable with, not the most a lender will approve. The cost calculator does the {state} property tax math for each county.
2. Check your credit reports. {#credit} Pull all three free at AnnualCreditReport.com, the site federal law sets up for this. Dispute errors now; fixes can take a month or more, and your score affects both your rate and your mortgage insurance.
3. Plan your down payment and cash to close. {#cash} Cash to close is the down payment plus closing costs and prepaid taxes and insurance. Keep the money where a lender can document it, and don't move large sums around without a paper trail.
4. Compare Loan Estimates from two or three lenders. {#loan-estimates} Ask each for an official Loan Estimate on the same day for the same loan, then compare the rate, the points, and the lender fees line by line. (Lenders)
5. Get a full pre-approval, not just a pre-qualification. {#preapproval} A pre-approval means a lender has checked your income, assets, and credit. Owners selling direct will ask for it before they take your offer seriously. (Lenders)
6. Ask about FHA, VA, and first-time buyer programs. {#loan-programs} FHA allows a smaller down payment, VA loans have no monthly mortgage insurance for eligible veterans and service members, and the Tennessee Housing Development Agency runs programs for qualifying buyers. A lender can tell you what you qualify for. (Lenders)
7. Choose your closing attorney or title company early. {#closing-pro} You'll need them to review or draft the contract, hold earnest money, search the title, and run the closing. Ask what they charge and what they need from you. (Attorneys)

## Section 2 — Search

8. Pick areas and look up their property tax. {#areas} Tennessee tax depends on the county and whether a home is inside city limits, so two homes at the same price can have very different bills. Use the calculator, then check the county trustee's site.
9. Set up listing alerts. {#alerts} Owners list when they're ready, and good homes go fast. Alerts email you the day a new home in your area is listed.
10. Tour safely and ask for the seller's disclosure up front. {#touring} Bring someone with you, tell a friend where you're going, and ask the owner for the Tennessee Residential Property Disclosure before you get attached to a house.
11. Verify the seller owns the property. {#ownership} Look up the owner of record on the county assessor's site or the state's property assessment search, and make sure it matches the person you're dealing with. Your title search confirms it before closing. (Attorneys)
12. Check the flood zone, HOA rules, and insurability. {#flood-hoa} Look the address up on the FEMA Flood Map Service Center, read any HOA covenants and fees, and get an insurance quote before you offer, not after. (Home Insurance)
13. Pull your own sold comparables. {#comps} Look for homes nearby that sold in the last few months with similar size, age, and condition. Sold prices, not asking prices. (We don't give pricing advice; this is how to find your own.)

## Section 3 — Make an offer

14. Have a real estate attorney draft or review the contract. {brand} doesn't write contracts. {#contract} Your attorney or title company can draft one or review the seller's, and explain every date and contingency before you sign. (Attorneys)
15. Set your price, contingencies, and deadlines. {#terms} Common contingencies cover inspection, financing, appraisal, and title. Each needs a clear deadline. Decide what you'll ask for and what you'll walk away over. (Attorneys)
16. Get the Tennessee Residential Property Disclosure, or understand what waiving it means. {#disclosure} Tennessee requires the owner to give either a disclosure of known material defects or, if the buyer waives the disclosure, a disclaimer that the home is sold as is. Ask your attorney before you waive anything. (Attorneys)
17. Name who holds the earnest money. {#earnest-holder} The contract should say a title company or attorney holds it in escrow, and when it's refunded or released. Never send earnest money to the seller directly, and never to {brand}; we never hold funds. (Attorneys)
18. Sign, deliver, and put every deadline on your calendar. {#sign} Once both sides sign, the clock starts. Write down each contingency deadline and who you need to call before it passes.

## Section 4 — Under contract

19. Deliver earnest money to the named holder. {#earnest-deposit} Get a receipt from the title company or attorney. Call them at a number you already have to confirm how to pay before you send anything. (Attorneys)
20. Schedule a home inspection right away. {#inspection} Tennessee requires home inspectors to be licensed. Go to the inspection, ask questions, and read the whole report, including what it says it doesn't cover. (Home Inspectors)
21. Order a termite (wood-destroying insect) inspection. {#termite} Home inspections usually don't cover termites. In Tennessee the report comes from a pest control company licensed for wood-destroying organisms. Ask your lender whether your loan requires one. (Pest Control)
22. Bring in specialists for anything the inspection flags. {#specialists} Roof, HVAC, foundation or crawlspace concerns deserve a specialist's look while you can still negotiate. (Roofers, HVAC, Foundation Crawlspace)
23. Negotiate repairs or credits in writing. {#repairs} Ask for specific repairs by licensed trades, a price credit, or both, and have your attorney put the agreement in writing. (Plumbers, Electricians)
24. Lock your rate and send your loan documents quickly. {#rate-lock} Respond to your lender's requests the same day, and don't open new credit or change jobs before closing. (Lenders)
25. Get the appraisal. {#appraisal} Your lender orders it. If it comes in under the price, your contract's appraisal terms decide what happens next. (Lenders)
26. Bind homeowners insurance. {#insurance} Your lender needs proof of coverage before closing. Ask about replacement cost and whether you need separate flood coverage. (Home Insurance)
27. Review the title search, title insurance, and survey. {#title} Your closing attorney or title company checks for liens and ownership problems. Ask about an owner's title policy and whether a survey is worth ordering. (Attorneys)

## Section 5 — Closing

28. Review your Closing Disclosure three business days before closing. {#closing-disclosure} Federal rules give you at least three business days with it. Compare it to your Loan Estimate and ask about anything that changed. (Lenders)
29. Confirm wiring instructions by calling your title company or attorney at a number you already had, never one from the wiring email. {#wire-fraud} Wire fraud targets home buyers with fake emails that look real. If instructions change at the last minute, stop and call. (Attorneys)
30. Do a final walkthrough. {#walkthrough} Within a day or two of closing, check that agreed repairs are done, the systems work, and nothing that stays with the house is gone.
31. Sign, fund, and get your keys. {#closing} Bring a government photo ID. Your closing attorney or title company records the deed with the county register of deeds. (Attorneys)

## Section 6 — Move-in handoff

32. Set up utilities and change your address. {#utilities} Water, electric, gas, internet, trash, and the post office. Some utilities need a deposit or a few days' notice.
33. Keep your closing file and start the new homeowner checklist. {#closing-file} Save the settlement statement, deed, title policy, inspection report, and warranties together. Then work through your first year as a homeowner.

Every purchase is different. Your real estate attorney, lender, and title company are the right people to answer questions about your contract, your loan, and your closing.
