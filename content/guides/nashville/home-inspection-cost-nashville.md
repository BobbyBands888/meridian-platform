There's no single price for a home inspection in Nashville. What you'll pay depends on the house, the inspector, and what you ask them to look at. Inspectors set their own fees; no government agency sets or publishes them. The only way to know today's price is to ask two or three inspectors for a quote on your specific home.

This guide explains what drives the price, what's usually extra, and how to compare quotes. It's general information, not legal advice. Sources were checked on September 17, 2026.

## Why it's worth paying for

A home inspection is your look at the house's condition before your inspection deadline passes. Tennessee's seller disclosure covers what the owner knows about; an inspection finds what they may not. When you're buying directly from an owner, it's often the main way repair requests and credits get negotiated.

Plan your inspection around the deadline in your contract. Our [buyer checklist](/buy/checklist?s=buyer_guide) puts it in order with everything else under contract.

## What drives the price

Inspection trade groups say the price depends on the inspector, the local market, and the scope of the job ([InterNACHI, home inspection FAQ, checked September 17, 2026](https://www.nachi.org/home-inspection-faq-buyers-sellers.htm)). In practice, expect quotes to move with:

- **Size.** Square footage is the most common starting point. More rooms, more systems, more time.
- **Age.** Older homes take longer: older wiring, plumbing, and roofing need a closer look.
- **Foundation type.** A crawlspace the inspector has to enter takes more time than a slab.
- **Extra systems.** More than one HVAC unit, a detached garage, a pool, or an accessory dwelling can add to the fee.
- **Distance.** Some inspectors charge more for homes farther from where they're based, which can matter in outlying parts of Middle Tennessee.
- **Timing.** A tight contract deadline or a weekend slot can limit your choices.

A HUD consumer guide makes a point worth remembering: price doesn't necessarily reflect quality ([HUD, "10 Important Questions to Ask Your Home Inspector"](https://files.hudexchange.info/resources/documents/10-Important-Questions-to-Ask-Your-Home-Inspector.pdf)).

## What a standard inspection doesn't cover

Tennessee's standards of practice for home inspectors say inspectors don't have to report on wood-destroying organisms, rodents, or insects, or on underground items, and the report must say it doesn't cover radon and other environmental hazards or underground sewage, water, or fuel systems ([Tenn. Comp. R. & Regs. 0780-05-12-.10, revised January 2026](https://publications.tnsosfiles.com/rules/0780/0780-05/0780-05-12.20260122.pdf)). That's why these are usually separate, and separately priced:

- **Termite (wood-destroying insect) inspection.** In Tennessee this report comes from a pest control company chartered for wood-destroying organisms, and the state says it's warranted for 90 days ([Tennessee Department of Agriculture, pesticide consumer tips](https://www.tn.gov/agriculture/businesses/pesticides/pesticide-consumer-tips.html)). Ask your lender whether your loan requires one.
- **Radon test.** Tennessee's environment department reports that about one in four Tennessee homes tested is above the EPA's action level of 4 pCi/L ([TDEC radon program](https://www.tn.gov/environment/stewardship/programs/radon.html); [EPA, updated November 12, 2025](https://www.epa.gov/radon/what-epas-action-level-radon-and-what-does-it-mean)). Many inspectors offer a test as an add-on.
- **Sewer line scope.** A camera look at the line from the house to the street. Worth asking about for older homes and homes with big trees in the yard.
- **Specialist follow-ups.** If the inspector flags the roof, HVAC, electrical, plumbing, or foundation, a licensed specialist can tell you what the fix involves while you can still negotiate.

When you ask for quotes, ask each inspector to price the same list: the base inspection plus the add-ons you want. Then you're comparing like with like.

## Check the license

Tennessee requires home inspectors to be licensed. It's unlawful to act as or advertise as a home inspector without one (Tenn. Code Ann. § 62-6-304). Licensed inspectors complete approved training, pass a national exam, and carry errors and omissions insurance ([Tennessee Department of Commerce & Insurance, Home Inspector Licensing Program](https://www.tn.gov/commerce/regboards/homeinsp.html)). You can look up any inspector's license at [search.cloud.commerce.tn.gov](https://search.cloud.commerce.tn.gov/).

## Questions to ask before you book

- Are you licensed in Tennessee, and what's your license number?
- What's included in your base price, and what costs extra?
- Will you go into the crawlspace and attic?
- Can I attend, and when will I get the written report?
- Do you offer termite, radon, or sewer scope services, or can you refer someone licensed for them?
