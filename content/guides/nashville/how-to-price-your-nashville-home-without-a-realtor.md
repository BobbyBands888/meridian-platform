Pricing is the decision that decides everything else. Price a Nashville home right and it draws showings in the first two weeks, when a listing gets the most attention it will ever get. Price it 5% high and it sits, buyers assume something's wrong, and the eventual sale is often lower than a correct price would have brought on day one.

Agents have two advantages here: MLS access and repetition. You can replace both with about two hours of work and the Davidson County property records. Here's how.

## Step 1: Find real comps, not online estimates

An online "Zestimate" or "Redfin Estimate" is a statistical guess that doesn't know your kitchen was renovated or that your lot backs up to a highway. In Nashville, where a 1940s cottage in 12 South and a 2019 tall-skinny in The Nations can sit two miles apart and sell for wildly different numbers per square foot, automated estimates are routinely off by 10% or more.

Comps are what buyers' agents and appraisers actually use. A good comp is:

- **Sold**, not listed. Asking prices tell you what sellers hoped. Sold prices tell you what buyers paid.
- **Within the last 90 days**, six months at most. Nashville's market moves; a comp from last spring is history.
- **Within half a mile**, or in the same subdivision. Nashville neighborhoods have hard edges. East Nashville's Lockeland Springs and Inglewood are next to each other and price differently.
- **The same style and era.** Ranch to ranch, tall-skinny to tall-skinny, new construction to new construction. A 1955 brick ranch is not a comp for a 2022 modern farmhouse on the same street.
- **Within 20% of your square footage** and the same bed/bath count, give or take one.

Three to five comps that meet those rules beat any algorithm.

### Where to find sold prices in Nashville

- **Zillow or Redfin, "Sold" filter.** Set the map to your neighborhood, filter to sold in the last 6 months, and read the actual closed prices, not the estimates. Both sites show sold data pulled from public records.
- **Davidson County Property Assessor** (padctn.org). Search by address to see the last sale price and date on any property, plus the assessed square footage. Williamson, Rutherford, Sumner, and Wilson counties have their own assessor sites with the same data.
- **Realtor.com "Recently Sold."** A third source to cross-check.

Write each comp on one line: address, sale date, sold price, square feet, beds/baths, and one note on condition if you can tell from the old listing photos ("renovated kitchen," "original 1970s baths").

## Step 2: Adjust for the differences

Comps are never identical, so appraisers adjust. You can do a simple version:

- **Price per square foot.** Divide each comp's sold price by its square footage. Across Nashville this figure varies widely by neighborhood, from the low hundreds per square foot in some areas to several times that in others; your own comps will show where your street sits. Your comps will cluster in a range; that range is your starting point.
- **Condition.** A comp with a new roof and renovated kitchen sold higher than yours would with 1990s finishes. Be honest here. Buyers will be.
- **Lot and location.** Corner lot, busy road, backing to a park, a walkable block in East Nashville, a cul-de-sac in Nolensville. These move prices by real percentages.
- **The one thing you can't see in photos.** Was it a bidding war? A comp that sold in three days with multiple offers may have gone above what the next similar home will bring.

Multiply your square footage by the per-square-foot number your adjusted comps point to. That's a range, not a price. Many homes sell within a few percent of that range, but there's no guarantee; an appraiser or your own judgment about condition should refine it.

## Step 3: Check what's competing with you right now

Sold comps tell you what the market paid. Active listings tell you what buyers will see next to yours this weekend. Search active homes within your comp criteria and note their prices and days on market.

Two things to look for:

- **A listing that's been sitting 60+ days** near your target price is a warning. Something about that price isn't working.
- **Two or three similar homes under contract in the last few weeks** at a certain price means the market has spoken. Price near them.

## Step 4: Read the Nashville market you're actually in

Nashville isn't one market. A few patterns that matter for a FSBO seller in 2026:

- **Middle Tennessee inventory is higher than it was in 2021–2022.** Buyers have choices, which means overpriced homes get skipped rather than "discovered."
- **Price reductions are common.** A large share of Nashville sellers cut their asking price at least once. Every reduction is public on Zillow and tells buyers you started too high. Better to be right the first time.
- **Suburbs and the city behave differently.** Franklin, Brentwood, and Nolensville hold value with families and school districts. Urban core neighborhoods swing more with interest rates and investor activity.
- **Season matters less than you think.** Spring has more buyers, but also more competition. A well-priced home sells in November.

## Step 5: Pick a number, and a strategy

You'll end up with a range. Where you land inside it is your decision, and reasonable sellers make different ones. The tradeoffs most sellers weigh:

- **Price at the low end** if you want a fast sale, expect multiple showings, and possibly competing offers. Many agents favor this approach; many sellers don't.
- **Price at the middle** if you're patient and the home shows well.
- **Price at the top** only if you've got something the comps don't (a renovated kitchen in a neighborhood of original ones, a rare lot) and you're willing to wait.

Whatever you pick, use a clean number that shows up in searches. Buyers search "$400,000 to $450,000," so a home at $449,900 appears in that search and $452,000 doesn't.

## Step 6: Plan your reassessment date before you list

The most expensive FSBO mistake is a slow series of small price cuts. Decide now: if there are no showings in 14 days, the price drops to a number you set today. If there are showings but no offers, it's the price or a visible defect; fix one or the other. One decisive move beats four small ones.

## When to bring in a professional

- **A pre-listing appraisal** costs $400–600 in Nashville and gives you a defensible number from a licensed appraiser. Worth it if your home is unusual or you and a co-owner disagree.
- **A pre-listing inspection** doesn't set the price, but it tells you what a buyer's inspector will find, so you can price the repairs in instead of negotiating them out later. [Find a Nashville home inspector](/vendors/home-inspectors).

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use the Zillow Zestimate to price my Nashville home?**
As a starting point, not as a price. Zestimates don't account for condition, renovations, or lot-specific issues, and Nashville's block-to-block variation makes them less reliable than in uniform suburbs. Three sold comps within half a mile will beat it every time.

**How do I find comps without MLS access?**
Zillow and Redfin's "Sold" filters, the Davidson County Property Assessor site, and Realtor.com all show closed sale prices from public records. The data is the same data agents see; you just have to filter it yourself.

**Should I price high to leave room to negotiate?**
Usually not. Nashville buyers filter by price range, so a home priced above its range doesn't get seen by the buyers who'd pay the most for it. Overpricing tends to produce fewer offers, not higher ones.

**How much does a pre-listing appraisal cost in Nashville?**
Typically $400–600 for a standard single-family home, more for large or unusual properties. Any licensed Tennessee appraiser can do one.

**What if my home is worth less than I owe?**
Then you're not ready to sell yet, or you need to talk to your lender about options. Get your payoff statement first (step 1 on the checklist) before you spend money on photos or repairs.
